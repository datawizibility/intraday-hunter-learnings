### Core idea
- Demand and supply **are** support and resistance — the same object, two vocabularies. The level is not drawn by geometry, it is identified by **where the market actually reacted**: reversal on the way up → **supply / resistance zone**; reversal on the way down → **demand / support zone**.
- A level is therefore a **zone**, not a line, and its job is decided by **the positions sitting in it** — fresh positions (not yet cut) make a level work; positions already cut make it stop working.
- From that follows this video's operational rule: **trade the zone's edge, not the inside of the zone** — either the reaction at the zone, or the break of the zone.
- And one discipline rule new to the corpus: **pick one of those two styles permanently.** Using both at once destroys your psychology (one day you read a breakout, the next a breakdown) — his explicit instruction is to choose one style and stay with it.
- He closes the video by telling the viewer **not to add extra science**: identify where the momentum/turn was seen, and that point *is* your support/resistance.

### Demand/supply = support/resistance (definitions he gives)
| Observed behaviour | What it becomes |
|---|---|
| Market in an up-move reverses and selling appears | **Supply zone = resistance** |
| Market in a continuous fall reverses and buying/momentum appears | **Demand zone = support zone** |

- Definition he states verbatim in substance: *"support zone and demand are those where we see a reaction"* — the zone is defined by the observed reaction, not by a measured level.

### The two ways to trade a level — and the one-style mandate
| Style | Mechanic | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| **Trade the reaction (level-fade)** | Price travels **to** the support zone → buy order, expect upside momentum; price travels to the resistance zone → sell | the touch and the reaction at the zone |
| **Trade the break** | Wait for the support to **break down**, then sell; wait for the resistance to **break out**, then buy | the break of the zone, then trade in the break's direction |

- ⚠️ **Never run both styles.** His reason is purely psychological: if you sometimes take the level trade and sometimes the breakout/breakdown trade, your read becomes self-contradictory — one day the market "looks like a breakout", the next "looks like a breakdown" — and the psychology breaks. Choosing one style permanently gives the better result.
- Both styles are legitimate; the rule is **consistency of style**, not the superiority of one.

### Level flip (breakout / breakdown)
- When a support or resistance is **broken, it reverses its role**:
  - broken **support** → becomes **resistance**; the next time price returns near that line, **selling** is seen there;
  - broken **resistance** (a breakout) → becomes **support**; the next time price returns there, it **helps as a support zone** and buying appears.
- He demonstrates both flips on the archive chart: a broken support re-labelled resistance (sold again on the retest), and a broken resistance re-labelled support.

### Why fresh levels work — the inventory reason
- The levels that work well for him are the ones where **positions have been freshly built and not yet cut**. Someone sold there and has not covered; someone bought there and has not cut; those positions carry stops.
- If the market returns to a place where **positions were already cut**, the level no longer delivers — the inventory that made it work is gone.
- Practical consequence he draws from it: work the **fresh** support/resistance; a level validated by fresh positions gives the good result.
- This is the same stock-pool logic as the earlier videos, applied to the drawing question — the line is a **map of who is holding**, which is why he insists the question is "where was the reaction?" and not "where is the pretty line?".

### Zones are prices, not closes
- He explicitly removes a common confusion: it is **not** necessary for the market to print a closing price at the level or to gap-open from it for the level to be valid.
- If the market simply **opens there and moves**, that place is still a demand/support zone (and the same on the supply side) — no gap-open is required for a resistance.
- So the zone is anchored to the **price point where the reaction happened**, not to a candle body, a close, or a gap.

### Drawing procedure (his sequence)
1. **Clear the chart.** Remove indicators (supertrend and similar overlays) so only price is visible — his stated reason is that you must be able to identify where demand and supply are.
2. **Find where the reaction came from.** Locate the points/areas where selling appeared in an up-move and where buying/momentum appeared in a fall.
3. **Mark it as a zone or a line.** A horizontal band (zone) or a single line are both acceptable; a band where the reaction was wide, a line where the point was clean.
4. **Mark the major zone** — identify where genuinely big selling/buying was seen; minor levels can also be logged ("small confusions are fine, no problem") but the major zone is the one that matters.
5. **Zoom in to refine.** He drops to the **5-minute** timeframe to see the finer reaction points and marks those too; on a lower timeframe the open/close of specific candles can be used as your line.
6. **Do not work inside the zone.** Once the whole area from one reaction point to the other is marked, the zone behaves as one resistance/support: work the **breakout** or the **rejection/failure** of the zone — buying and selling *inside* it creates problems.
7. **Where the big buying/selling was printed** — that is where you identify the zone and act; not a discretionary mid-zone trade.

### Keep permanently
1. **Demand = support, supply = resistance, and both are defined by where the market actually reacted** — not by geometry.
2. **Pick one trading style and keep it:** trade the reaction at the level **or** trade the level's break. Running both kills your psychology; he says consistency of style is the better result.
3. **Broken levels flip:** broken support becomes resistance (sold on the retest); broken resistance becomes support (bought on the retest).
4. **A level works because of positions, not lines.** Fresh levels — where positions were built and not yet cut — work; levels whose positions were already cut stop working.
5. **Do not trade inside the zone.** Mark the zone, then work its **break** or its **failure/rejection**; mid-zone trading is where the trouble starts.
6. **Zones are price-anchored, not close-anchored:** no closing print and no gap-open is needed for a zone to be valid — an open inside it that moves is enough.
7. **Clean the chart first:** remove indicators (supertrend etc.) so price and zones are visible — identification comes before decoration.
8. **Draw where the reaction came from**, band or line, major zone first; then drop to a lower timeframe (5-minute) to refine, using candle opens/closes at that scale if useful.
9. **Minor levels are allowed; don't over-engineer.** His instruction: no extra science — find where the turn/big buying/selling printed; that is your S/R.
10. **Levels drawn fresh carry into the next session** — the same zones remain usable in the following day's market.
