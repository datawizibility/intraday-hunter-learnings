### Core idea
- This session answers an execution-hygiene question that every projection trader faces and that the earlier lessons never specified: **how much history do you study?** One day's chart, two days', or a whole expiry — and once an expiry is complete, do you keep following it?
- His answer: **you follow the chart of the expiry you are trading (the fresh one).** When an expiry completes and the next chart opens, you follow that one — and **mostly one day is enough**, because when you go to the next expiry you can see that **positions have already been built there**, which is exactly what you need in order to know **where the stop-losses will be available.**
- He allows **one to two days** and, rarely, more — but the wider lookback is **condition-gated**: he names the exception only conceptually here (a chart where he expects a continuation of a particular size) and explicitly defers the general rule to a future live session, so this note does not invent one.
- The stop-loss question is then turned into a **position-management** exercise: you decide your own exit from **your own analysis**, while reading *the counterparty* off the chart — and the general rule is stated: **once a chart has already eaten its stop-losses, stop following it**; but the **first approach** to a level is definitely followed.
- He closes with the momentum mechanism that makes it all work — *"the market does not stop"* belief pulls in positions that should never have been taken, and then the market comes to eat **their** stops — plus the demand that the student check all of this on a real chart, not just listen.

### The lookback rule (how many days, and which expiry)
- The frame: you are working on **projection**, so you must know **how the counterparty is building his positions** — that is the thing that tells you where the stops are. Hence the chart window is not a study preference, it is an input to the SL read.
- **Follow the new expiry's chart.** If an expiry completes and a new chart opens, that is your reference: "when you go there you will see the position is already built — so definitely follow that."
- **Mostly one day.** He is explicit that one day is enough for the normal case: "most of the time you should follow one day — one day's chart is enough", because positions get built in the new expiry.
- **One to two days** is permitted when a condition appears on the chart; he shows the shape of that condition conceptually (e.g. a chart where he expects a continuation of a certain size) and says he will demonstrate the general condition when he next records on live charts. **The general rule is deliberately left open in this session.**
- Where he touches a longer view (position built in the *earlier* expiry when a continuation is expected), he immediately restricts it: **targets there are kept small** — fewer points, no big target — "maximum one day" remains the working norm.

### Position management — decide your side, then read their side
- The setup he walks through: a **sell** position, the market gives profit up to a point, and the question is whether to **cut or hold**.
- First instruction: **decide it by your own analysis — "do not think about what the other side is doing"** when managing your own position.
- Second step is the separate inference about the counterparty: if the buyer saw profit and then the market came back and **reduced that profit** (i.e. travelled back through his gain), then the buyer has **definitely cut**; and **if the market crosses the first channel low, he has cut for certain** — whether he intended to or not.
- Why it matters to him: those market actions tell you **whose stops are still sitting** and whose are gone. Reading the inventory the market has already consumed is what stops you from targeting a side that has no fuel left.

### Follow a chart until its stops are eaten
- ⭐ The rule, stated in his own words: *"Now this chart has already eaten the stop-losses, so now I will not follow this chart. But when the market approached here **the first time**, I would definitely follow that side."*
- So the sequence is: **first approach to a level = follow it (it is the trade)**; after the pool is consumed = **the chart is finished** and following it is wasted work.
- Reinforcing principle: **someone who has already exited cannot be hunted.** "The market cannot go to eat him, because the market goes where the **higher probability** is." (This is the comparative pool rule from 2022-03-20, applied over time rather than across two sides.)

### Reading where the stops sit on the chart
- **At the top of the move.** His instruction for locating the target pool: you look for the buyer/seller stops **right above the top** — because as soon as the market formed that top and the selling started, someone put money in expecting a reversal, and **their stops are parked there**.
- **Where selling began** is the same reference point: "here someone placed his money to reverse the market" — that is the entry inventory whose stops are now the objective.
- **Closing prints mark stop locations.** He adds that whether the market gives a **red closing or a green candle** at a level — consider it; that is where **both buyers' and sellers' stops** are found.
- **The momentum print does not create the pool — it reveals it.** His question "why does that momentum come?" is answered below.

### The momentum mechanism (why the fast print appears)
- ⭐ *"Everyone feels the market is not going to stop, so even those who should not take a position start taking positions — and when the market comes to eat **their** stop-losses, they are forced out at the border."*
- The fast momentum print is therefore **not a new force**: it is the market **manufacturing the crowd** that will supply the next pool of stops.
- The **post-momentum check** he gives (ASR is rough here — reproduced, not sharpened): after the momentum, do not study the momentum candle — look at the **lower level**: is the stop-holding/participation there or not? If it is there, no problem; if it is **not** there, there is a problem and **the market can come to eat the down-side stop-losses.** See the fidelity section.
- **Slow projection days:** if the market is building a good projection **slowly**, with a level or support visible where stops are forming, the market **will not rush** — it will track/grind them and *then* start moving into its position. (Cross-check with the 2022-04-02 "slow = temptation" rule; see Evolution.)
- **Closing learning demand:** listen, then **go to the chart yourself** and see how it actually behaves. "I will tell you buy-sell, but you have to see how the chart has to be followed" — his final instruction on the specifics is: don't look at the shape; look at the **one particular part**, where the buyer/seller saw a profit and their position got cut — *there* the stops will not be available to you.

### Keep permanently
1. **Follow the fresh (new) expiry's chart** — the one where positions are already being built; that is where the stop inventory you are going to trade against actually lives.
2. **Mostly one day is enough.** One to two days when a specific condition appears; longer lookbacks are exceptions that must be justified on the chart, not habit.
3. **Study enough history to answer one question: where will the stop-losses be available?** If the lookback does not answer that, you are reading for entertainment.
4. **⭐ First approach to a level = follow and trade it. Once the stops there are eaten, stop following that chart.** A consumed chart has no target left.
5. **Someone who has already exited cannot be hunted** — the market moves toward the **higher probability** of available stops, not toward a memory.
6. **Manage your own position on your own analysis** — never on a guess about what the other side is doing; then read *their* state separately off the chart (profit given back / channel low crossed ⇒ that side has cut).
7. **Read stop location off the chart**: at the top of the move / where selling began, and at a level's **closing print (red or green)** — that is where both sides' stops sit.
8. **The momentum print is manufactured, not organic:** "the market will not stop" pulls in positions that should not exist, and those become the next pool of stops to be eaten.
9. **On a slow projection day the market will not rush** — it grinds/tracks the level where stops are forming and moves afterwards; do not force an entry inside that grind.
10. **Verify every one of these reads on a real chart.** Listening is not learning; the specific part he tells you to study is *where a side's position was cut* — because there the stops are no longer available to you.
