### Core idea
- The session is a **loss post-mortem framework**: when a loss happens, what exactly do you audit? He answers with three common mistakes, then applies the framework to **his own loss** in public.
- The chain he wants the trader to see: **wrong entry → target missed or late → the trade starts badly → patience never develops → the whole trading process degrades.** Entry accuracy is therefore treated not as a preference but as the root variable of the entire process.
- Two causes of bad entries, both behavioural rather than analytical:
  1. **You were distracted** — trading while busy with office work or conversation. Focus is not on the chart, the state of mind is not balanced, "that seat"/psychology activates, and the loss follows.
  2. **You missed the first entry and then improvised** — the market gave the move immediately, you lacked the courage to enter (even having seen the chart), you waited for a retracement, and the market took your stop-loss instead. One missed opportunity then cost him **one of his own three rules**.
- The frame for the fix: **2–3 written rules, followed completely.** Emotions cannot be "finished" — "you are a human being, not a machine, you will have emotions" — they can only be **reduced**, and the reduction comes from rules plus discipline, not from willpower on the day.
- The closing lesson is scale: **make a big target in small steps** (₹1,000 → ₹1 lakh → 2 → 5 → 10 lakh → ₹1 crore) and **fix small repeated mistakes** rather than searching for one large change.

### Mistake 1 — trading while occupied
- If you place trades while busy (at work, mid-conversation, "in the middle of other things"), your attention is not fully on the chart. In that state the mind is **not balanced for trading**, and the trader's own psychology is what activates — hence the loss.
- His instruction: **trade only when you have the time to handle the position and watch the chart.** The mistake is not the setup; it is the mental availability at the moment of execution.
- He returns to this mistake at the end with his **own** loss: he had been busy elsewhere, looked at the chart in a hurry, and *"even my analysis per the setup went bad"* — the same distraction, one level deeper.

### Mistake 2 — entry accuracy (the diagnostic)
- Definition he works from: a **setup** is written — *where* to enter, *where* the stop-loss goes, *how* to read it (5-minute or 1-minute). With that written, a loss becomes auditable.
- **The test:** after the entry, if the market first shows you roughly **half your stop-loss in heat** and then your target is achieved, your analysis was approximately accurate — and **once or twice is fine**, keep using the setup.
- **The failure pattern:** if again and again the market first gives you the loss-side reading and only afterwards the target, **your entry point is wrong** — not your analysis, not your luck.
- **The tolerance band** (his percentages, as spoken): an entry that is 10–20% off can still be tolerated — "even 30%, up to 40% I can accept that the market will come back to your side" — but if the market keeps stopping you out and *then* delivering the target, the entry point itself is wrong.
- **Why it cascades:** a wrong entry means a broken target; a broken target means the trade starts badly; a trader who repeatedly starts badly never develops patience with time; and without that patience the overall trading spoils — he notes that patience cannot be built by a trader who cannot even hold his entry consistent.
- **Options add a clock:** if you are an **option buyer**, the longer the market takes to reach your target, the worse the trade is for you (time decay). A fast target is a good trade; a slow target is evidence your entry was misplaced. He states this as the reason entry accuracy matters *more* for buyers.
- **Remedy he gives for this failure:** **wait** — let the market move a little away first so the entry lands at a better point, and the target then comes without the same pain.

### Mistake 3 — the missed entry and the broken rule (the case study)
- The trader he describes had waited for a **breakdown** to enter. The market gave it immediately and ran. He had seen the chart, but "his courage did not build" to take the entry at that moment.
- Unable to wait for the clean entry, he then improvised: drew a **line** on the retracement, waited for the retest, and took the position there — the market did break down, **but it took his stop-loss** (the retest entry was the trap).
- The root cause as he states it: **he had missed the first entry**, so he became emotional, and in that state he **broke one of the three rules** he had made for himself (his rules concerned waiting for the daily/attending the channel — ASR-rough). One missed opportunity produced one loss and one broken rule.
- ⭐ The durable instruction: **write 2–3 rules and follow them completely.** A missed opportunity is *not* a reason to abandon a rule mid-week; that is exactly how a system becomes inconsistent.
- And the realistic note: **emotions cannot be eliminated** — "you are human, not a machine; emotions will remain" — they can only be **reduced**, by having rules and following them in a disciplined way. That is his stated substitute for willpower.

### His own loss, analysed in public
- **The error: the entry point was wrong.** Had he taken the entry at the **final point**, the market would have given him the momentum; instead he received momentum in the **wrong direction** (his spoken figure is ASR-unclear — see the fidelity section). Because the entry was wrong, his target broke and he had to take the loss.
- **What produced the wrong entry:** he had only **started following that chart a short time earlier**, so his read was under-developed; he judged that the market was "creating too much pressure", let that perception decide, and took a **down-side** trade on the expectation that the market would print down-side momentum to create sentiment. His own words: had he not thought that way, his decision would have been different.
- **The precondition he uses before every entry — momentum size.** His plan needs the market to be able to deliver **100–150 points**; he takes the entry only when he can see that much momentum available. If he judges the market may go the other way (or deliver more on the other side), **he does not enter.** Here he entered expecting ~100 points without the second half of the check.
- **The check he skipped — the buy-side stops.** ⭐ His explicit admission: *"I did not see whether the nearby buy-side [stops] were available."* At that moment enough stops were not available on that side, so the market "tried" and then went the opposite way — the target pool simply was not there.
- **What he could have done:** **wait for the market to move a little further first**, so his entry sat at a better point, and then the target would have come; the momentum afterwards would not have been a problem. The read was not the failure — the **price he paid for it** was.
- He also notes in passing that because he looked at the chart in a hurry (while busy), **his analysis-per-setup was compromised too** — the mistake-1 mechanism appearing inside his own example.

### Scale: big result, small steps
- A large target — in trading or in life — must be completed in **small steps**: first ₹1,000, then ₹1 lakh, then 2, 5, 10 lakh, and only then the crore. "If on the first day you think you will earn a crore, however much money you put in, you will not earn it."
- Daily-goal numbers ("₹1,000 a day") are illustrative: he warns the same rule does not apply to everybody — the transferable point is the **stepwise build**, not the exact figure.
- **Fix small mistakes, not one big thing.** If you believe becoming professional/profitable requires one large change, "you are thinking completely wrong." Small repeated errors are what keep a trader from earning; avoiding and not repeating them is the work.
- Closing observation: "the other man also fixed his own mistakes" — the market does not owe you money; you earn it by correcting the small errors you keep repeating.

### Keep permanently
1. **Never trade while occupied.** If you cannot give the chart your time and full attention, the state of mind is wrong and the loss is the setup's smallest part.
2. **Entry accuracy is the root variable**: wrong entry → broken/late target → bad start → no patience → degraded process. Audit the entry first, before the analysis.
3. **Diagnostic for a bad entry:** if the market **repeatedly** shows you heat before the target, or takes too long to reach it, the entry point is wrong — not your luck, not the method.
4. **Tolerance band:** an entry 10–20% (even 30%) off can still be recovered; if the market keeps stopping you out and *then* delivering the target, accept ~40% as the limit and judge the entry wrong.
5. **Option buyers: time is a cost.** The longer the market takes to reach the target, the worse the trade; a fast target is evidence of a good entry.
6. **Momentum-size precondition:** take the entry only when the chart can deliver the momentum you need (his working figure: 100–150 points). If the other side can deliver more, there is no trade.
7. **⭐ Check the stops before the entry** — the failure that produced his own loss was not reading whether the buy-side stops were available. No pool, no trade.
8. **Wait, don't force.** If the market has not yet moved away from your entry, giving it room improves the entry price and the target arrives without the pain.
9. **Write 2–3 rules and follow them completely.** A missed opportunity never justifies breaking a rule; emotions cannot be eliminated, only reduced — rules are the reduction mechanism.
10. **Build big targets in small steps** (₹1,000 → 1 crore) and **fix small repeated mistakes** — profitability comes from removing recurring small errors, not from one large change.
