### Core idea
- This is the **counterpart video to the setup itself**: he says plainly that *knowing when I do NOT work on my setup is more important for you than knowing how I work on it*, because **using the setup wrongly can cause loss.** His stated motive: "if my content doesn't benefit somebody, I don't mind — but my content must not cause somebody's loss."
- The whole session is an **exception list around one condition**: the setup works where stop-losses are **available** on the side the market is travelling to, and it must **not** be used after a momentum print has already consumed that side's stops.
- This is the first session in the corpus that turns the stop-inventory thesis into an explicit **"do not trade" condition** rather than a "where to trade" condition.

### The condition — when NOT to use the setup
- Setting: **after a big momentum has already happened.** The stop-losses on one side have been eaten. The trade everyone now wants to take (the recovery trade, e.g. a **call-side** position after heavy selling) is exactly the one he rules out.
- His mechanism, as spoken: the market first eats one side's stops, then the other side's — *that is the only way it gives the big momentum*. Once a side's inventory has been eaten, the market can no longer be expected to run there, and **a position taken in the hope of recovery can produce a very large loss.**
- His warning to himself and to the viewer: **do not decide that the market will not go against your chosen direction.** If a sentiment (negative or positive) has formed, **a trend change takes time** — it does not flip because you are positioned for it.
- ⚠️ He adds a money-management consequence: in the aftermath of such a move his own levels would sit at **quite different places** than usual, because the fresh positions were built earlier and the inventory is already spent — so the usual level map is not valid in that window.

### ⭐ The recovery-after-selling trap
- After a good, extended sell, the market does sometimes **show a recovery** — and he flags that recovery as the trap: it is the print that pulls in the counter-trend position. (His Hindi wording here is ASR-degraded — see the fidelity section — so the *name* he gives it is not reproduced, only the mechanism.)
- He is explicit that **failing to avoid it can cost far more than you expect** — in that specific configuration he says he had not imagined a loss that large was possible.
- Conversely, a **sell-side setup inside real selling is not the problem** — that is the setup working normally. The problem is the position that assumes the recovery.

### ⭐ Others' stop-losses matter more than your own
- The line he repeats twice in this session: **your own stop-loss you can always decide yourself — set it according to your own target. What you must know is where *other people's* stop-losses are.**
- If you know the counterparty's stop location, then "your **entry** will be good, your **exit** will be good, your **target** will be better." The counterparty's stop is therefore treated as an input to entry/exit placement, not as trivia.
- He says he will keep returning to this, and that he deliberately shows **how other people place their stops** — which he rates as more important than where he personally puts his.

### Targets are capped by the stop inventory
- He describes stopping his own trade **before his stated target** because he knows **no stop-losses are available beyond that point** — even if the market then continues, he will not hold for more, because there is nothing left ahead to fuel the move.
- General form: a target that requires the market to travel into an **empty** area is not a target. Experience is what lets you read how far the fuel lasts; at the start, decide only "this much position, this much target, and out."

### Moves you cannot read — do not force them
- He admits openly that **he does not always understand why the market moved** a particular way on a given chart, and that a random person's pattern will sometimes be unreadable.
- The explanation he gives for "unexplained" selling: markets have been running for **ten, twenty years** — someone who bought long ago finally **books his target** (a large quantity offered into the market), or a piece of news is already **priced in** before you see it. Those prints have reasons you have no access to.
- Instruction: **do not try to understand every move. Work on the moves you DO understand** — and specifically, work where the **stops are clearly visible**. Where they are not visible it is a slightly **advanced** situation: no need to operate there yet; you will learn it going forward. He says it twice: *first work where the stop-loss is clearly visible.*

### Entry discipline — the "a little late" trade-off
- ⭐ Before entering, assess **how far the market could travel on the opposite side** (his phrasing: how much the sentiment can carry). To avoid being caught by that move, **enter slightly late.**
- He states the price of the early entry plainly: **first entry ⇒ bigger stop-loss and smaller target.** Waiting costs you the best price but buys you the correct side. His earlier entry-quality rule (2022-04-17) is the same trade-off measured after the fact.
- Entries are not one technique: support/resistance, breakout, gap, gap-close, sentiment, trend — "there are two or three ways," and he himself uses a combination. He started from **price** and added the others gradually.
- ⭐ **Do not burn your old setup to adopt one new thing.** If you work on support/resistance, also keep a glance on the trend line and on the setup; if you worked with indicators, keep a little of that. But abandoning your existing setup entirely for a single new tool is the mistake he names explicitly ("never make that mistake — wherever you learned it from, this is the process").

### Start small — his large figures are not a beginner's plan
- He uses his own large targets as an example and then warns against copying them: if someone starts out aiming at figures that size, then when the trade goes to **−6** (his phrasing, ASR-unclear unit) he will be demotivated, go into "depression", and the **mindset** — which he calls the one thing that decides long-term benefit — degrades.
- His instruction: **use small steps first**, then the bigger target comes. If you learn from him, take the *direction*, not the number.
- He also notes he cannot compress himself into one or two hours of video: he shares what he thinks will be useful, when he notices a mistake worth correcting.

### Not every day is yours
- ⭐ Closing rule of the session: **"Intraday Hunter trades every day, so I must too" is the wrong inference.** If nothing is clear that day, there is no obligation to trade that day. The obligation is to know *when* the setup applies.

### Keep permanently
1. **Know when NOT to use the setup** — that is more valuable than knowing how to use it. Using it wrongly is how this method loses money.
2. **Never take the counter-trend (recovery) position after a momentum print has already consumed that side's stops** — the market eats one side, then the other; a spent side has no fuel left.
3. **Do not decide that the market will not go against your direction**: once a sentiment has formed, changing the trend takes time.
4. **Others' stop-losses outrank your own**: set your own stop from your own target, but find **their** stop — it is what makes the entry, the exit and the target better.
5. **A target is bounded by available fuel**: if no stops remain beyond a point, exit before your stated target — the market has nothing left to give there.
6. **Trade only where the stops are clearly visible**; where they are not, it is an advanced situation — no need to operate there yet.
7. **Do not force an explanation for every move**: long-held positions booking targets and already-priced news produce prints with causes you cannot see. Work on the moves you can read.
8. **Enter a little late on purpose**: the first entry gives a bigger stop-loss and a smaller target — that is the price of the correct side.
9. **Layer your tools, never replace them**: keep the setup that works while adding the new one; abandoning your existing method for a single new technique is the named mistake.
10. **Not every day is yours** — if nothing is clear, there is no obligation to trade that day. Take the direction from a teacher, never his figures.
